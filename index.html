First, the fact. Raise the rate and the price falls — later payments are divided more times, so they give up more. Not proportionally: the curve bends.

Second, the tool. On a bent curve, getting from one point to another takes a series of corrections, each smaller than the last. That series is called the Taylor expansion. Above: flat, sloped, bent — the third rides the black curve so closely that only the extremes separate them.

P(r+Δr)  =  P(r)  +  P′(r) Δr  +  12P″(r) Δr²  +  16P‴(r) Δr³  +  …

Tab 1 is the first correction. Tab 2 is the second.

Show the derivation
Build the answer one correction at a time. Each level removes the error the level above it left behind.
Guess 0P(r+Δr) ≈ P(r)
Assume the price does not move at all. The error is the entire change.
Guess 1… + P′(r) Δr
At the point where you start, the price changes at rate P′. Apply that rate across the whole distance Δr. What is left over is caused by P′ not holding still along the way.
Guess 2… + 12P″(r) Δr²
P′ does not hold still — it changes at rate P″. This term corrects for exactly that. The ½ is 1/2!, and the paragraph at the bottom says where it comes from.
Guess n… + 1n!P(n)(r) Δrn
Each level costs one more derivative and one more power of Δr. Because Δr is small, Δrn shrinks quickly, so the list can be cut short without much loss.
Where n! comes from. Every time you differentiate a power, the exponent drops in front as a factor and the power goes down by one. Do it n times and those factors multiply up:
ddΔr  Δrn  =  n Δrn−1
d²dΔr²  Δrn  =  n(n−1) Δrn−2
dndΔrn  Δrn  =  n(n−1)(n−2)…1  =  n!
So write the answer as c₀ + c₁Δr + c₂Δr² + … and differentiate n times at Δr = 0. Terms below n are gone. Terms above n still carry Δr and vanish. Only cₙ is left, and it arrives multiplied by that accumulated n!. Dividing it back out is the only reason the coefficient is 1/n!.

Why divide by P? P′ alone is measured in price units. Dividing by P converts it into a percentage per unit of rate, which compares across any two bonds.

Show the derivation
the bondP  =  Σ CFt(1+r)t
one paymentddr 1(1+r)t  =  − t(1+r)t+1
all of themP′  =  − Σ t CFt(1+r)t+1
Differentiate the sum one payment at a time. Nothing else has happened yet.
split it(1+r)t+1  =  (1+r)t × (1+r)
The (1+r)t half turns CFt back into PVt, the value already sitting in the table. The lone (1+r) depends on no t, so it comes out of the sum:
 P′  =  −Σ t PVt1 + r
÷ by P−P′/P  =  Σ t wt1 + r
Dividing by P turns every PVt into its share wt.
Σ t wt is the average wait, each year weighted by how much of the price that payment carries. It is called Macaulay duration. Nobody assumed it — it appeared, because differentiating a power drops the exponent out in front. Divide it by 1+r and you get modified duration, the number that multiplies the rate move.

one paymentd²dr² 1(1+r)t  =  t(t+1)(1+r)t+2
Differentiating twice drops t, then t+1, and pushes the exponent up by two.
all of themP″  =  Σ t(t+1) CFt(1+r)t+2
split it(1+r)t+2  =  (1+r)t × (1+r)²
Same move as before. (1+r)t rebuilds PVt; the (1+r)² carries no t and leaves the sum:
 P″  =  Σ t(t+1) PVt(1 + r)²
÷ by PP″/P  =  Σ t(t+1) wt(1 + r)²
Differentiating twice brings down t, then t+1, and leaves two powers underneath. That is the whole of convexity. The ½ standing in front of it in the expansion is the Taylor coefficient 1/2!, nothing more.

Walk the same distance down, then the same distance up. The gain beats the loss, every row.
MoveRate fallsRate risesSum2 × term 2

That is convexity in money: when the rate falls the price gains more than it loses when the rate rises by the same amount. A straight line would put 0.0000 down the whole Sum column. Adding the two moves cancels the first-order part, so the surplus that is left is exactly twice term 2 — the second derivative, measured by hand.

Nothing above is an estimate of the price. Each piece is a named part of the same total — so they must add back to it exactly. Here they are, added back.

What this leaves out
  • One flat rate for every payment, rather than a real yield curve
  • Annual coupons; most real bonds pay twice a year, which changes the numbers but not the structure
  • Settlement on a coupon date, so no accrued interest
  • No credit risk, no tax, no calls or prepayment
