The yield sits under every payment. Raise it and every payment shrinks — and the later the payment, the more times it gets shrunk. That is the whole reason the curve above falls, and falls in a bow shape.

The last column weights each year by how much money it carries. Add it up: 7.9894. The bond pays you, on average, 7.99 years from now — not 10, because the coupons along the way pull the average forward.

This average waiting time has a name: Macaulay duration. It is measured in years, because it is an average of years.
2Longer wait, bigger price move

Why does waiting time matter for price? Because a payment 8 years away gets shrunk 16 times by the yield, while one 6 months away gets shrunk once. The longer you wait, the more times a yield rise bites you. So the average wait is almost exactly the price sensitivity — it just needs one small adjustment:

7.9894  ÷  (1 + 0.05/2)  =  7.7946

The division by 1.025 knocks off one half-year of shrinking — the very first one, which affects every payment equally. Nothing deeper than that.

The result has a name too: modified duration. Read it as a speed: the price moves 7.79% for every 1% the yield moves — downhill when yields rise.

2Why? Because the slope refuses to stay put

When yields rise, every payment is worth less — and a bond that is worth less has less left to lose. So the curve gets flatter out to the right, and steeper out to the left. The short dark tick on the chart is the slope where you are standing right now. The blue line froze the slope at its starting value and used it the whole way.

And the drift always works for you. The bond goes numb exactly while you are losing, and turns extra sensitive exactly while you are gaining. That one sentence is the entire secret of the gap.

3So the gap is: half the drift, times the move

The drift starts at zero and builds steadily across the move — so on average, the slope you used was off by half the total drift. And the drift itself grows with the move: double the move, double the drift. The move therefore enters twice. That is all Δy² means.

The drift rate 73.6287 is what people call convexity. Because the drift always helps, this correction is always added — whichever way yields went.

Seen ½ a t² in school physics? Same shape. Duration is your speed; convexity is your acceleration.

4Where 73.6287 comes from — same table, one new column

Duration weighted each payment by year. The drift rate comes from weighting each payment by year × (year + ½) instead — the far payments count much more, because they are the ones whose sensitivity changes fastest. (The extra ½ is one payment period; it appears because the shrinking happens in half-year steps.)

One last touch-up, the same one duration needed — except duration divided by 1.025 once, and this divides twice. Duration was about one round of shrinking; the drift is about shrinking on top of shrinking, so the adjustment happens twice.

What this leaves out
  • One flat yield for every payment, rather than a real yield curve
  • Settlement sits exactly on a coupon date, so there is no accrued interest
  • No credit risk, no tax, no embedded options such as calls or prepayment
  • Yields move in parallel — the curve does not steepen or flatten
